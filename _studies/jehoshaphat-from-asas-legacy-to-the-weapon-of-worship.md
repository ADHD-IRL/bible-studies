## Introduction: The Legacy of King Asa (15 Minutes)

**Read Aloud:** 2 Chronicles 14:11 and 16:7-9, 12

To understand Jehoshaphat, we must first look at the father who raised him. King Asa was a reformer who started his reign with fierce devotion to God. When faced with a massive, overwhelming Cushite army early in his reign, Asa prayed a beautiful prayer of dependence: *"Lord, there is no one like you to help the powerless against the mighty."* God delivered them, and Asa spent years tearing down idols and calling Judah back to faith.

However, Asa did not finish well. Decades later, when faced with a much smaller border dispute with King Baasha of Israel, Asa didn't pray. Instead, he emptied the temple treasury to buy a mercenary alliance with the King of Aram (Syria). When a prophet rebuked him for relying on human politics rather than God, Asa became enraged and threw the prophet in prison. He ended his life bitter, physically afflicted by a severe foot disease, and refusing to seek God's help.

Jehoshaphat inherited his father's kingdom and a powerful cautionary tale: starting strong is not the same as finishing strong.

**Discussion Questions:**

1. Early in his life, Asa relied entirely on God to face a massive army. Later, he relied on his own wealth and political maneuvering to solve a smaller border dispute. Why is it sometimes harder to trust God with our "everyday" problems after years of experience than it was when we were younger and more desperate?
2. Asa's spiritual reforms were largely negative: he tore down altars, smashed idols, and removed his idol-worshiping grandmother from power. As we will see, Jehoshaphat went a step further by *teaching the Word*. Why isn't it enough to just stop doing bad habits (tearing down idols) without replacing them with positive spiritual disciplines?

## Part 1: Fortifying the Foundation (35 Minutes)

**Read Aloud:** 2 Chronicles 17:1-10 and 19:4-7

Jehoshaphat's early reign was defined by internal fortification rather than external conquest. He learned from his father that military strength was useless if the nation was spiritually hollow. By dispatching a mobile educational unit of princes, priests, and Levites, he brought the Book of the Law directly to the people. Later, he established a decentralized judicial system, instructing his judges that their verdicts were not merely legal decisions, but divine responsibilities.

Because of this internal spiritual fortitude, the "fear of the Lord" fell on surrounding kingdoms, bringing an unprecedented era of peace and economic prosperity to Judah.

**Discussion Questions:**

1. Jehoshaphat believed national security began with spiritual education. In our own lives, how often do we try to build "walls" (financial security, career success, relationships) without first addressing our foundational spiritual literacy?
2. Look at 2 Chronicles 19:6-7. Jehoshaphat tells his judges, "Consider carefully what you do, because you are not judging for mere mortals but for the Lord." How would applying this exact mindset to your current job or daily responsibilities change the way you work?

## Part 2: The Peril of Pragmatism (35 Minutes)

**Read Aloud:** 2 Chronicles 18:1-4, 18:28-34, and 21:1-6

Despite his wisdom, Jehoshaphat had a fatal blind spot, and in some ways, he repeated his father's late-in-life mistake of relying on human alliances. He craved unity so much that he sacrificed his convictions to get it. He allied with King Ahab of Israel, one of the most corrupt rulers in biblical history. To cement this political treaty, Jehoshaphat married his son Jehoram to Ahab's daughter, Athaliah.

When Ahab asked Jehoshaphat to join him in the battle of Ramoth-Gilead, Jehoshaphat agreed. He was nearly killed by archers who mistook him for Ahab. Worse, the marital alliance he arranged out of "pragmatism" eventually resulted in his daughter-in-law Athaliah introducing Baal worship directly into Judah's royal family, eventually murdering nearly all of Jehoshaphat's grandchildren to seize the throne.

**Discussion Questions:**

1. Jehoshaphat's alliance with Ahab was likely motivated by a good desire: peace between the divided sibling kingdoms of Israel and Judah. When have you justified a bad partnership or compromised a boundary because you had "good intentions"?
2. In the battle of Ramoth-Gilead, Ahab disguises himself but tells Jehoshaphat to wear his royal robes, effectively making him a target. How does this illustrate the reality of what happens when we align ourselves with toxic or unprincipled people?

## Part 3: The Weapon of Worship (35 Minutes)

**Read Aloud:** 2 Chronicles 20:1-12, 15-17, and 21-24

Toward the end of his reign, a massive, multi-national coalition marches on Judah. Unlike his father Asa, who panicked late in life when faced with an attack, Jehoshaphat remembers to seek God. He cannot rely on his wealth, his fortified cities, or political alliances. He calls a national fast and stands before his people, offering a brutally honest prayer: *"We have no power... We do not know what to do, but our eyes are on you."*

Through the prophet Jahaziel, God responds: "The battle is not yours, but God's."

Jehoshaphat's response is highly unorthodox. He places a choir at the front of the army. They don't sing a war chant; they sing a song of Thanksgiving. As they praise, the enemy coalition turns on itself. Judah's army arrives only to collect the plunder.

**Discussion Questions:**

1. Leaders usually project absolute confidence in a crisis. Jehoshaphat stood before his entire nation and admitted, "We do not know what to do." Why is this kind of vulnerability actually a sign of spiritual strength rather than weakness?
2. Read verse 12 again. *"We do not know what to do, but our eyes are on you."* What current situation in your life requires you to pray this exact prayer?
3. The choir was sent *ahead* of the army. Praise preceded the victory. How does actively expressing gratitude and worship in the *middle* of a crisis change your perspective, even before the problem is resolved?

Take the final 10 minutes to share prayer requests, specifically focusing on areas where the group feels "outnumbered" and needs to shift their eyes back to God.
