## Overview

John 14:27 was spoken on the darkest night the disciples had known. Judas had gone out to betray Jesus, Peter had just been told he would deny Him three times, and Jesus was speaking about leaving them. Into that fear Jesus said, "Peace I leave with you, My peace I give to you; not as the world gives do I give to you. Let not your heart be troubled, neither let it be afraid." This study looks at what kind of peace Jesus gives, how it differs from the peace the world offers, and why that peace is meant to make believers bold. The same Jesus who gave peace in the upper room later stood among His frightened disciples behind locked doors, said "Peace to you!" and sent them out.

## Theological Analysis

**A peace left as an inheritance.** "Peace I leave with you" uses language a person might use of a will. Jesus was about to die, and this is part of what He left behind. He had no house, money or land to give His friends. He gave them His peace. It is not a mood He hopes they will find. It is a gift He hands over, and it belongs to them because He gave it.

**"My peace."** Jesus does not give a general calm. He gives His own peace, the peace He had with the Father. That matters because Jesus was not untouched by distress. John says, "Now My soul is troubled" (John 12:27) and, at the supper, "He was troubled in spirit" (John 13:21). The same word for "troubled" appears in 14:27. Jesus knew sorrow and dread, yet He walked toward the cross with settled trust in the Father. The peace He gives is not the absence of trouble. It is His own steadiness in the middle of it.

**Not as the world gives.** The world offers peace in three common forms. It offers peace by escape, avoiding whatever disturbs us. It offers peace by control, securing ourselves with money, power or approval. It offers false peace, saying all is well when it is not. The prophets condemned leaders who cried, "Peace, peace!" when there was no peace (Jeremiah 6:14). Jesus gives peace by a different route. He first makes peace between us and God. "Having been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:1). Peace of heart grows out of that settled peace with God, so it cannot be taken away by circumstances.

**A command to troubled hearts.** "Let not your heart be troubled, neither let it be afraid." Jesus gives a gift and then gives a command. We are not passive. Believers are told to refuse to let fear rule the heart, because they have been given something stronger than fear. The phrase repeats John 14:1, where the remedy is trust: "you believe in God, believe also in Me." Peace is received by trusting the One who gives it.

**The Spirit who brings His peace.** Jesus' promise of peace comes right after His promise of the Holy Spirit (14:26). Jesus would no longer be with the disciples in the body, but the Helper would come, teach them, and remind them of all that Jesus said. The peace of Christ is carried to us by the Spirit of Christ, the same Spirit who gives power, love and a sound mind (see [Spirit of Power]({{ '/studies/2-timothy-1-7-spirit-of-power-2026-11-26/' | relative_url }})).

**Peace that sends.** After the resurrection, the disciples were behind shut doors "for fear of the Jews" (John 20:19). Jesus stood among them and twice said, "Peace to you!" Then He said, "As the Father has sent Me, I also send you" (20:21). Christ's peace did not keep them hidden. It opened the doors. The peace Jesus gives is the very thing that frees believers to speak about Him in an unstable world.

## Historical & Cultural Context

Jesus spoke these words in the upper room in Jerusalem during Passover week, around AD 30 or 33, hours before His arrest. John 13 to 17, often called the Upper Room or Farewell Discourse, records His last teaching to the disciples before the cross.

"Peace" was a common Jewish greeting and farewell, from the Hebrew shalom. Shalom meant far more than quiet. It meant wholeness, well-being and right relationship with God and others. When Jesus said, "Peace I leave with you," He took a familiar parting word and filled it with new meaning.

The Roman world boasted of the Pax Romana, the peace of Rome. It was a real stability, but it was kept by legions, crucifixions and fear of Caesar. Roman peace was imposed from outside and could be lost in a day. Jesus' words, "not as the world gives," stand in sharp contrast. His peace is given from within, through a cross that Rome itself would carry out.

## Practical Application

1. Read John 14:27 slowly each morning this week and receive it as a gift Jesus has already given you, not a feeling you must produce.
2. Name one place where you are seeking peace "as the world gives," whether by escape, control or pretending all is well. Bring it to Jesus in prayer.
3. When your heart begins to be troubled, answer it with John 14:1: "believe in God, believe also in Me."
4. Ask the Holy Spirit to remind you of Jesus' words when fear rises, especially before a hard conversation.
5. Think of one person who needs to hear about the peace of Christ. Let His peace, not your nerves, set the tone when you speak with them.
6. Let the peace of God rule in your heart (Colossians 3:15) in your church and family. Settle a conflict this week if you can.

## Greek & Hebrew Word Study

1. **"eirēnē" [Greek G1515]: "peace"**
   Peace, harmony, wholeness. In the New Testament it often carries the full meaning of the Hebrew shalom: being made whole and right with God.

2. **"shalom" [Hebrew H7965]: "peace"**
   Completeness, soundness, welfare, peace. It was the everyday greeting of Israel and the word behind Jesus' farewell.

3. **"aphiēmi" [Greek G863]: "leave"**
   To send away, to leave behind, to let go. In John 14:27 it pictures Jesus leaving His peace with the disciples as He departs.

4. **"tarassō" [Greek G5015]: "be troubled"**
   To stir up, agitate, unsettle, like water churned up. It is used of the pool of Bethesda being stirred and of Jesus' own distress in John 12:27 and 13:21.

5. **"deiliaō" [Greek G1168]: "be afraid"**
   To be timid, to shrink in fear. This verb appears only here in the New Testament. It is related to the noun "deilia" in 2 Timothy 1:7, the spirit of fear God has not given us.

6. **"paraklētos" [Greek G3875]: "Helper"**
   One called alongside to help, an advocate or comforter. The Spirit is the One who stays beside believers and carries Christ's peace to them.

## Apologetics & Deeper Study

The disciples' change from fear to courage is one of the strongest historical facts in the New Testament story. On the night Jesus spoke John 14:27 they scattered, and after the crucifixion they hid behind shut doors. Weeks later the same men were preaching openly in Jerusalem, the city that had just executed their Lord. Something gave them a peace that fear could not shake. The Gospels say it was the risen Jesus standing among them and saying, "Peace to you!"

The verse also answers a modern question: Is Christian peace only a coping technique? Many methods aim to calm the mind. Jesus' peace is different in its source. It rests on a real event, His death and resurrection, and on a real relationship, peace with God through justification (Romans 5:1). Its strength comes from what is true, not only from how we feel.

### Common Objections & Responses

1. **Objection:** "If Christians have Christ's peace, they should never feel anxious."

   **Response:** Jesus spoke these words to men who were about to feel deep fear, and He Himself was troubled in spirit. His peace is not the absence of every anxious feeling. It is a gift that holds the heart steady and can be received again and again.

2. **Objection:** "This kind of peace is just positive thinking with religious words."

   **Response:** Positive thinking depends on the thinker. Christ's peace depends on Christ. It rests on His finished work and His promise of the Spirit, not on our ability to control our thoughts.

3. **Objection:** "With the world as unstable as it is, being at peace means ignoring reality."

   **Response:** Jesus' peace looks straight at reality. He spoke it hours before His own death. It does not deny trouble. It trusts the One who rules over it, and it sends believers into that troubled world with good news.

### Supporting Evidence

1. **The Setting of the Farewell Discourse**
   John 13 and 14 place these words after Judas' departure and the prediction of Peter's denial, showing the peace was given in a moment of real fear.

2. **The Disciples' Transformation**
   John 20:19-21 and the book of Acts record the disciples moving from locked rooms to open witness after meeting the risen Jesus.

3. **The Contrast with Roman Peace**
   The Pax Romana was maintained by force, a well-documented feature of first-century Roman rule, which gives weight to Jesus' words "not as the world gives."
