## Overview

Moses was dead, and Joshua stood at the edge of the Jordan with a nation to lead into a land full of walled cities and strong enemies. God's first words to him were not a battle plan. Three times in four verses God told him, "Be strong and of good courage." This study looks closely at the Hebrew words behind that command and at what God gave Joshua to make it possible. Joshua's courage was not a personality trait. It rested on God's promise, on God's Word in his mouth and on God's presence wherever he went. Those same supports give believers courage to live and speak for Jesus in uncertain times. It follows the group lesson [Fear Not]({{ '/studies/isaiah-41-10-fear-not-2026-11-24/' | relative_url }}).

## Theological Analysis

The command comes three times, and each time it is tied to a different support.

**Courage grounded in God's promise (v. 6).** "Be strong and of good courage, for to this people you shall divide as an inheritance the land which I swore to their fathers." Joshua's courage did not rest on the size of Israel's army but on an oath God had made centuries earlier to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. The outcome was already settled in God's word. Courage grows when we remember that God's purposes do not depend on our strength.

**Courage fed by God's Word (vv. 7-8).** The second command is stronger: "be strong and very courageous." Surprisingly, the courage God names here is not for battle but for obedience: "that you may observe to do according to all the law." It takes great courage to keep God's Word when everyone around you is going another way, and to turn neither "to the right hand or to the left." Verse 8 shows how that courage is built. The Book of the Law was not to "depart from your mouth," and Joshua was to "meditate in it day and night." The Word of God in the mouth and on the mind is the fuel of courage. People who are silent about God's truth are often people who have not been filled with it.

**Courage sustained by God's presence (v. 9).** The final command begins with a question: "Have I not commanded you?" Courage is not optional for God's servants. Then come two prohibitions: "do not be afraid, nor be dismayed." The reason is the heart of the passage: "for the LORD your God is with you wherever you go." It repeats the promise of verse 5: "as I was with Moses, so I will be with you. I will not leave you nor forsake you." Joshua would face nothing alone.

**Courage that spreads (v. 18).** At the end of the chapter the people answer Joshua with God's own words: "Only be strong and of good courage" (1:18). Courage received from God becomes courage passed on to others. This connects to the theme of speaking boldly about Jesus. Believers who are strengthened by God's promise, Word and presence become people who strengthen others. Paul gives the same charge to the church: "be strong in the Lord and in the power of His might" (Ephesians 6:10), and "be brave, be strong" (1 Corinthians 16:13).

## Historical & Cultural Context

Joshua had served as Moses' assistant for forty years. He was one of the twelve spies who explored Canaan, and with Caleb he urged Israel to trust God and enter the land. Their words then were, "do not fear the people of the land... the LORD is with us. Do not fear them" (Numbers 14:9). The people refused, and that generation wandered in the wilderness until it died. Now Joshua stood where Israel had stood forty years earlier, facing the same land, the same fortified cities and the same temptation to fear.

The setting makes God's repeated command deeply personal. Joshua had seen what fear did to a whole generation. He had also seen God's faithfulness through the wilderness. The command to be strong and courageous was a call to choose the path the previous generation refused, trusting that the God who had been with Moses would be with him.

## Greek & Hebrew Word Study

1. **"chazaq" [Hebrew H2388]: "be strong"**
   To be firm, to grow strong, to hold fast. It pictures something that holds its grip and does not let go. It is the strength to stand firm under pressure.

2. **"amats" [Hebrew H553]: "be of good courage, be courageous"**
   To be alert, bold and steadfast. Paired with chazaq, it describes inner resolve. God Himself promises to supply this strength in Isaiah 41:10, using the same root.

3. **"me'od" [Hebrew H3966]: "very"**
   Exceedingly, greatly. In verse 7 it intensifies the command. Obeying God's Word fully requires even more courage than facing enemies.

4. **"hagah" [Hebrew H1897]: "meditate"**
   To murmur, mutter or speak quietly. Biblical meditation is not emptying the mind but reading God's Word aloud and turning it over until it fills the heart and comes out of the mouth.

5. **"arats" [Hebrew H6206]: "be afraid"**
   To tremble, to be terrified, to be struck with dread. God forbids this paralyzing fear because He is present.

6. **"chathath" [Hebrew H2865]: "be dismayed"**
   To be shattered, broken down or discouraged. It describes the collapse of spirit that follows fear. God's presence keeps His servants from breaking under pressure.

7. **"andrizomai" [Greek G407]: "be brave"**
   Used in 1 Corinthians 16:13. Literally to act with manly courage, to show maturity and steadfastness. The New Testament carries Joshua's charge into the life of the church.

## Apologetics & Deeper Study

Joshua 1 shows that biblical faith is not blind optimism. God does not tell Joshua that the task will be easy or that the enemies are weak. He tells him the truth about the challenge and then gives him reasons for courage that rest outside himself. Faith in Scripture is trust in a God who has spoken and acted in history, beginning with His promises to the patriarchs and continuing through the exodus and the wilderness.

The passage also anticipates Jesus. The name Joshua and the name Jesus are the same name in Hebrew and Greek, meaning "the LORD saves." Joshua led God's people into an earthly inheritance. Jesus leads His people into an eternal one, and He makes the same promise of presence: "lo, I am with you always, even to the end of the age" (Matthew 28:20). Hebrews 13:5 applies the promise first given to Joshua directly to Christians: "I will never leave you nor forsake you."

### Common Objections & Responses

1. **Objection:** "You cannot command someone to have courage. Either they feel it or they do not."

   **Response:** God's command is not a demand to manufacture a feeling. It is a call to act in trust because of who God is. Courage in Scripture is obedience in the presence of fear, supported by God's promise, Word and presence.

2. **Objection:** "The promise of success in verse 8 sounds like a prosperity gospel."

   **Response:** The success promised is tied to obeying God's Word and fulfilling His purpose, not to personal wealth. Joshua faced battles, setbacks and hard labor. Prosperity here means the accomplishment of God's mission, not a life free of difficulty.

3. **Objection:** "This was a special word for a military leader, not for ordinary believers."

   **Response:** The same words were spoken to all Israel (Deuteronomy 31:6) and repeated by the people to Joshua (Joshua 1:18). The New Testament gives the same charge to the whole church (1 Corinthians 16:13, Ephesians 6:10), and Hebrews 13:5 applies Joshua's promise to every believer.

### Supporting Evidence

1. **A Repeated Charge Across Generations**
   "Be strong and of good courage" appears in Deuteronomy 31:6-7 and 31:23, Joshua 1:6-9 and 1:18, and later in David's charge to Solomon (1 Chronicles 22:13, 28:20), showing a consistent pattern of courage grounded in God's presence.

2. **Joshua's Own Life**
   Numbers 14 records Joshua urging Israel not to fear the people of the land, and the book of Joshua records him leading Israel into the land forty years later, showing courage formed over a lifetime of trusting God.

3. **The New Testament Echo**
   Hebrews 13:5 quotes the promise first given to Joshua, and Paul's charges in Ephesians 6:10 and 1 Corinthians 16:13 carry the same call into the church.
