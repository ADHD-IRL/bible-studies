**בְּרֵאשִׁית**

## Part One: The Word Itself

בְּרֵאשִׁית: *bereshit*, accented on the final syllable: be-ray-SHEET.

### Morphology

| Element | Form | Function |
|---|---|---|
| בְּ | be | Inseparable preposition: in, at, with, by |
| רֵאשִׁית | reshit | Feminine noun: beginning, first, chief part |
| Root | ראשׁ (r-'-sh) | From רֹאשׁ (rosh), "head" |

### Semantics

*Reshit* is not a neutral time-marker. Built on *rosh*, "head," it carries priority and rank as much as chronology. The same noun describes:

- **Firstfruits:** the sheaf of *reshit* waved before the Lord (Lev 23:10), the best and first of the harvest
- **Israel:** "the *reshit* of His increase" (Jer 2:3), holy to the Lord
- **Wisdom:** "the fear of the Lord is the *reshit* of knowledge" (Prov 1:7), where the sense is foundational and chief, not merely earliest
- **Strength:** Reuben as Jacob's *reshit ono*, the firstfruits of his vigor (Gen 49:3)

So the opening word means something closer to *the first and foremost of a thing* than *the moment before which nothing occurred*. Hold that, because it matters at the end of this study.

## Part Two: The Grammatical Problem

### What Is Missing

There is no definite article.

"In *the* beginning" would require בָּרֵאשִׁית (*ba-reshit*), with a qamets under the *bet* absorbing the article. What the Masoretic Text gives us is בְּרֵאשִׁית with a shewa: anarthrous, no article at all.

This is the whole problem in one vowel point.

### Why It Matters

Hebrew nouns appear in two states:

- **Absolute:** the standard, independent form. "A beginning." "The beginning," if the article is present.
- **Construct:** a bound form that means "beginning *of* ___." It cannot stand alone. It requires whatever follows to complete it, and construct nouns never take the definite article themselves.

*Reshit* has the same consonants and vowels in both states, so the form alone does not settle it. But the missing article is suggestive, because a construct noun is exactly what would lack one.

If *bereshit* is **absolute**, verse 1 is an independent sentence:

> In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth.

If *bereshit* is **construct**, verse 1 is a dependent temporal clause hanging on what follows:

> When God began to create heaven and earth, the earth being unformed and void, God said, "Let there be light."

Two very different openings to the Bible, from one absent vowel.

## Part Three: The Case for the Construct Reading

### 1. Every Other Occurrence Is Construct

*Bereshit* appears five times in the Hebrew Bible. Four of them are unambiguously construct:

| Reference | Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Jer 26:1 | bereshit mamlekhut Yehoyaqim | In the beginning of the reign of Jehoiakim |
| Jer 27:1 | bereshit mamlekhut Yehoyaqim | Same |
| Jer 28:1 | bereshit mamlekhut Tsidqiyah | In the beginning of the reign of Zedekiah |
| Jer 49:34 | bereshit malkhut Tsidqiyah | In the beginning of the reign of Zedekiah |

The fifth is Genesis 1:1. On pure distribution, the construct reading has every other data point in the canon.

### 2. Rashi's Argument

Rashi (11th c.) states it flatly: the verse does not teach the order of creation, and if it did it would have been written *bari'shonah*. He argues *reshit* is everywhere in construct, and cites Hosea 1:2 as the decisive parallel:

> *techillat dibber YHWH be-Hoshea*: "At the beginning of the LORD's speaking through Hosea"

Here a construct noun (*techillat*, "beginning of") is followed directly by a **finite verb** (*dibber*, "he spoke") rather than a noun. That is precisely the construction required for *bereshit bara* to mean "in the beginning of God's creating."

Rashi then takes verse 1 as the temporal clause, verse 2 as parenthetical, and verse 3 as the main clause.

### 3. The Ancient Near Eastern Pattern

Mesopotamian creation texts characteristically open with a dependent temporal clause. *Enuma Elish* begins "When on high the heavens had not been named..." and the main clause arrives several lines later. *Atrahasis* opens the same way. E. A. Speiser, in the Anchor Bible *Genesis*, leaned heavily on this and rendered the verse "When God set about to create heaven and earth."

The force of the argument is that Genesis 1 would be using a recognized genre convention, and doing so in order to subvert it.

### 4. Modern Translation Committees

- **NJPS (1985):** "When God began to create heaven and earth,"
- **NRSV:** "In the beginning when God created the heavens and the earth," with the alternative in a footnote
- **CEB, NABRE:** similar dependent renderings
- **Ibn Ezra** reached the same conclusion independently of Rashi

## Part Four: The Case for the Absolute Reading

### 1. The Masoretic Accentuation

This is the strongest single piece of evidence and the one most often skipped.

The Masoretes placed a *tipcha* on בְּרֵאשִׁ֖ית. Tipcha is a **disjunctive** accent. It separates the word from what follows.

Had they understood *bereshit* as construct, bound to *bara*, they would have marked it with a conjunctive accent joining the two. They did not. The scribes who preserved and pointed the text, and who knew the grammar as a living inheritance, read verse 1 as an independent clause.

### 2. The Verb Form Is Wrong for a Clean Construct

To get "when God began to create," Hebrew would more naturally use an **infinitive construct**: *bero Elohim*, "in God's creating," exactly the construction found in Genesis 5:1 (*beyom bero Elohim adam*) and 2:4 (*behibbare'am*).

Instead we have בָּרָא, a finite perfect. Construct-before-finite-verb does occur, as Hosea 1:2 shows, but it is the marked and unusual option. The writer had a clean way to say "when God began to create" and did not use it.

### 3. Anarthrous Temporal Nouns Are Normal

Hebrew frequently omits the article on temporal expressions where English requires one. *Me-rosh*, *mi-qedem*, and similar forms function definitely without the article. Isaiah 46:10 offers *reshit* itself in an apparently absolute use:

> *maggid me-reshit acharit*: "declaring the end from the beginning"

So the missing article, while suggestive, does not compel the construct reading.

### 4. The Ancient Versions Are Unanimous

Every ancient translation read it as absolute:

| Version | Rendering |
|---|---|
| Septuagint | Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἐποίησεν ὁ θεός: finite verb, independent clause |
| Vulgate | In principio creavit Deus |
| Targum Onkelos | be-qadmin: "in the beginning" |
| Peshitta | Independent clause |

These are the earliest interpreters we have, all working in a Semitic or Semitic-adjacent environment, and none of them heard a dependent clause.

### 5. Syntactic Weight

If verse 1 is subordinate, the Bible opens with a lengthy, awkward periodic sentence spanning three verses, with a parenthesis inside it. If verse 1 is independent, it is a summary title followed by the detailed account, which matches the structure of Genesis elsewhere: statement, then elaboration.

## Part Five: Where the Weight Falls

Both readings are grammatically defensible. Neither is a translator's fabrication, and anyone who tells you the question is settled is overstating in one direction or the other.

My assessment: the **absolute reading is better supported**, primarily on the accentuation and the verb form. The Masoretic pointing is not decorative, and the availability of *bero* as a cleaner construction is difficult to explain away. The construct reading rests largely on distribution and on an ANE genre argument that assumes what it sets out to show.

But hold the conclusion loosely, and teach both. The construct reading is not a liberal invention. Rashi held it in the eleventh century and had no modernist axe to grind.

## Part Six: What Is Not at Stake

The construct reading is sometimes said to concede eternal pre-existent matter, and therefore to dissolve *creatio ex nihilo*. Two corrections.

**First, it does not follow.** "When God began to create, the earth was unformed and void" describes the condition of what God had made at the point the narrative picks up. It does not assert that the unformed material was uncreated or eternal. The clause locates the camera, not the metaphysics.

**Second, the doctrine does not rest on this verse.** Creation from nothing is carried across the canon:

- **Hebrews 11:3:** what is seen was not made from things that are visible
- **Romans 4:17:** God calls into existence the things that do not exist
- **John 1:3:** all things came into being through Him, and apart from Him not one thing came into being
- **Colossians 1:16:** all things, visible and invisible, were created through Him and for Him
- **Psalm 33:6, 9:** He spoke, and it was

Even if the construct reading won outright, none of that moves.

One thing that does not move in either reading: **the verb בָּרָא (*bara*)**. In the Qal stem it takes **God as its subject, always, without exception in the Hebrew Bible**. No human being ever *bara*. Whatever the syntax of the opening clause, the verb itself marks the act as categorically divine.

## Part Seven: The Oversized Letter

In the Masoretic manuscripts the opening ב is written **larger than the surrounding text**, one of the *litterae majusculae*, a deliberate and preserved scribal marking.

The rabbinic reading of it: *bet* is the second letter of the alphabet, not the first, and its shape is closed on top, bottom, and right, open only to the left, the direction of reading. Revelation begins where it begins. What lies behind and above is closed to us. The book opens with a door facing one way.

Take that as homiletical rather than exegetical. But it is not a modern invention, and it is a fair observation about the posture Genesis asks for.

## Part Eight: Ἐν ἀρχῇ

The Septuagint rendered *bereshit* as Ἐν ἀρχῇ, *en archē*.

John 1:1 opens with exactly those two words.

That is not coincidence, and it is not subtle. John writes a second Genesis and expects a reader who knows the first. He then keeps the vocabulary running:

- **John 1:3:** all things came into being through Him, answering *bara*
- **Colossians 1:18:** Christ is the ἀρχή, the *reshit*, and the firstborn from the dead
- **Colossians 1:15:** πρωτότοκος, firstborn, which is the *reshit ono* of Genesis 49:3 applied to Christ
- **Revelation 3:14:** the ἀρχή of the creation of God
- **1 Corinthians 15:20, 23:** Christ the ἀπαρχή, the firstfruits, which is the *reshit* of Leviticus 23:10

Return now to Part One. *Reshit* never meant only "earliest." It meant *first in rank, chief, the best and foremost portion, the firstfruits*.

The New Testament does not merely allude to the word that opens the Hebrew Bible. It assigns that word, in its full semantic range, as a title to a person.

## Discussion Questions

1. Why would the Masoretes' accentuation carry evidential weight that a modern grammarian's judgment does not? What are the limits of that argument?
2. Rashi says the verse "does not come to teach the order of creation." What does he think it comes to teach, and does the construct reading serve that aim?
3. If Genesis 1 deliberately borrows the opening convention of *Enuma Elish*, what would it be doing with it? Is imitation here agreement or polemic?
4. Trace *reshit* through Lev 23:10, Jer 2:3, Prov 1:7, and Gen 49:3. How does the range of the word change how you hear Genesis 1:1?
5. Does the doctrine of creation from nothing stand or fall on Genesis 1:1? Which passages would you build it on if this verse were disputed?
6. The verb *bara* takes God as subject every time it appears in the Qal. What does the writer accomplish by using a verb no human can be the subject of?
7. John opens with the Septuagint's rendering of the first two words of Genesis. What is he claiming, and what would be lost if he had opened with anything else?
