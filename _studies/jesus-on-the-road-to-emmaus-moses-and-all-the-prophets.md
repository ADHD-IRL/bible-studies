In Luke 24, Jesus delivers the definitive masterclass on biblical theology, fundamentally reshaping how His followers read the Hebrew Scriptures. When He meets the two despondent disciples on the road to Emmaus, they are disillusioned because they expected a political conqueror who would overthrow Rome, not a crucified teacher. Jesus does not immediately reveal His physical identity; instead, He reconstructs their theology to prove that the cross was not a tragic interruption of the Messianic plan, but its necessary climax.

While Luke's gospel does not provide the exact transcript of this conversation, the apostolic preaching in the Book of Acts, which is directly built on the hermeneutics Jesus taught that day, allows us to see exactly how He would have used the Law and the Prophets to reveal Himself.

## Correcting the Messianic Paradigm

Jesus begins with a profound rebuke:

> "O foolish ones, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken! Was it not necessary that the Christ should suffer these things and enter into his glory?"

Luke 24:25-26

The disciples suffered from a selective reading of Scripture. They had embraced the prophecies of a triumphant, reigning King (like Daniel 7 and Psalm 2) but completely ignored the prophecies of the Suffering Servant. Jesus had to show them that **the crown of glory could only be achieved through the cross of suffering**.

## "Beginning with Moses" (The Torah)

Luke records that Jesus began "with Moses," meaning the first five books of the Old Testament. He likely walked them through the foundational typologies of redemption:

- **The First Gospel (Genesis 3:15):** He would have shown that the promised Seed of the woman had to have His heel bruised (the physical death of the crucifixion) in order to crush the serpent's head (defeating sin and death).
- **The Provision of the Ram (Genesis 22):** Just as God provided a substitute ram caught in the thicket so that Abraham's son Isaac would not have to die, God had now provided His own Son as the ultimate substitute for humanity.
- **The Passover Lamb (Exodus 12):** Jesus would have explained that the Exodus was a physical shadow of a spiritual reality. Just as the blood of the unblemished lamb saved the Israelites from the angel of death, His blood was shed to deliver humanity from the ultimate judgment of sin.
- **The Bronze Serpent (Numbers 21):** Jesus likely reminded them (as He earlier told Nicodemus) that just as Moses lifted up the bronze serpent in the wilderness to heal the dying Israelites, the Son of Man had to be lifted up on a Roman cross so that whoever looked to Him in faith would be healed of their sin.
- **The Levitical Sacrifices:** He would have demonstrated that the endless river of animal blood in the temple was never meant to permanently remove sin, but merely to serve as a placeholder pointing to the one, final, perfect sacrifice He had just accomplished.

## "And All the Prophets"

Moving into the prophetic literature, Jesus would have illuminated the explicit texts predicting a rejected, suffering Messiah:

- **Isaiah 53:** He would have walked them line-by-line through the "Man of Sorrows," showing how He was "pierced for our transgressions" and "crushed for our iniquities," making it clear that the Messiah's death was a penal substitution, not an accident of history.
- **Psalm 22:** He would have pointed to David's prophetic lament, detailing the piercing of hands and feet, the casting of lots for garments, and the agonizing cry of abandonment ("My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"), all of which they had witnessed just days prior.
- **Zechariah 12:10:** He would have shown them the prophecy where God says the inhabitants of Jerusalem will "look on me, on him whom they have pierced," and mourn for Him as one mourns for an only child.

## Revelation in the Breaking of Bread

The intellectual exposition prepared their hearts (they later noted, "Did not our hearts burn within us while he talked to us on the road, while he opened to us the Scriptures?") but it was not until they sat at the table that their eyes were opened.

When Jesus took the bread, blessed it, broke it, and gave it to them, He assumed the role of the host. This action deliberately echoed the feeding of the multitudes and, more importantly, the Last Supper, where He established the broken bread as the symbol of His broken body. It was at the exact intersection of **understanding the Word** and **experiencing the sacramental fellowship of the broken bread** that the disciples finally recognized the resurrected Christ.
