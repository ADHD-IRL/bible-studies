Joseph was the 11th son of Jacob (who was later renamed Israel), and his narrative occupies nearly one-quarter of the book of Genesis. His story begins with him being the favorite son of Jacob, which caused jealousy among his brothers.

Joseph's story is told in Genesis chapters 37-50, where we see how God's plan worked through difficult circumstances to preserve the budding nation of Israel during a severe famine.

Here's how Joseph's journey led to Israel's establishment:

## Family Conflict and Joseph in Egypt

Joseph's brothers were jealous of him because he was their father's favorite, and Joseph had dreams suggesting his family would bow down to him. This jealousy culminated in a terrible act:

His brothers sold him into slavery, and he was taken to Egypt. While this appeared to be a tragedy, it set in motion God's plan for preserving Jacob's family.

## Joseph's Rise to Power

In Egypt, Joseph faced both trials and opportunities:

After being falsely accused and imprisoned, Joseph interpreted dreams for Pharaoh about seven years of plenty followed by seven years of famine. Because of his wisdom and divine insight:

Pharaoh appointed Joseph as second-in-command over Egypt, putting him in charge of storing food during the seven plentiful years to prepare for the coming famine.

## Reunification with Family and Migration to Egypt

When the famine struck, it affected the entire region:

Jacob's sons traveled to Egypt to buy grain, not knowing they would encounter their brother Joseph. After testing his brothers and seeing they had changed, Joseph revealed his identity.

Joseph invited his father Jacob (Israel) and the entire family to move to Egypt, where they could survive the famine under his protection. This migration was crucial for Israel's development.

## Israel in Egypt

Jacob's family settled in Goshen, a fertile region of Egypt, where they prospered and multiplied. This fulfilled part of God's covenant with Abraham that his descendants would become a great nation.

Jacob (Israel) blessed Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh before his death, adopting them as his own sons and giving them tribal inheritance among his other sons. This expanded Israel from 12 to 13 tribes (though typically still referred to as the 12 tribes of Israel, with Joseph's inheritance divided between his two sons).

## The Long-Term Significance

Joseph's story demonstrates how God worked through human events to fulfill His promises to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. By bringing Israel to Egypt, God:

1. Protected them from the famine
2. Provided a place where they could grow into a nation
3. Fulfilled His prophecy to Abraham that his descendants would be strangers in a foreign land

Although Joseph's death concludes Genesis, his impact continued as the Israelites remained in Egypt for approximately 400 years until the time of Moses. During this period, they grew from a family of about 70 people into a nation of hundreds of thousands.

## From Joseph to Moses: Israel's Journey to the Promised Land

### The Oppression in Egypt

After Joseph's death, the Israelites continued to live in Egypt for generations. As time passed, "a new king arose over Egypt who did not know Joseph" (Exodus 1:8). This pharaoh became fearful of the growing Israelite population and their potential loyalty to Egypt's enemies.

The Egyptians subjected the Israelites to harsh slavery, forcing them to build supply cities like Pithom and Rameses. Despite this oppression, the Israelite population continued to grow, which led Pharaoh to order the killing of all newborn Hebrew boys.

### Moses' Early Life

During this time, a Levite woman gave birth to a son and hid him for three months. When she could no longer hide him, she placed him in a basket among the reeds of the Nile River. Pharaoh's daughter found the baby, named him Moses (meaning "drawn out"), and raised him as her own in the Egyptian royal household.

Moses grew up with the privileges of Egyptian royalty but was aware of his Hebrew heritage. After killing an Egyptian who was beating a Hebrew slave, Moses fled to Midian where he lived for 40 years, married, and became a shepherd.

### The Call of Moses

While tending his father-in-law's flocks near Mount Horeb, Moses encountered God in a burning bush that wasn't consumed by the flames. God commissioned Moses to return to Egypt and lead the Israelites to freedom, promising to bring them to a land flowing with milk and honey: the land promised to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

### The Exodus from Egypt

Moses returned to Egypt and, along with his brother Aaron, confronted Pharaoh with God's demand: "Let my people go." When Pharaoh refused, God sent ten plagues upon Egypt. The final plague, the death of the firstborn, led to Pharaoh finally releasing the Israelites.

The Israelites left Egypt in haste, taking their unleavened bread dough with them. This hasty departure is commemorated in the Jewish festival of Passover. The Israelites, now numbering around 600,000 men plus women and children, began their journey toward the promised land.

### The Wilderness Journey

God led the Israelites with a pillar of cloud by day and a pillar of fire by night. When Pharaoh changed his mind and pursued them, God parted the Red Sea, allowing the Israelites to cross on dry ground while the Egyptian army was destroyed by the returning waters.

The journey through the wilderness lasted 40 years, during which:

- God provided manna and quail for food
- Moses received the Ten Commandments and other laws at Mount Sinai
- The Tabernacle was constructed as a portable sanctuary
- The people frequently rebelled against God and Moses

Because of their lack of faith when spies reported on the promised land, God decreed that the generation who left Egypt (except for Joshua and Caleb) would not enter the promised land. They would wander in the wilderness until that generation died off.

### Entering the Promised Land

After Moses' death, Joshua became the new leader of Israel. Under his leadership:

- The Israelites crossed the Jordan River into Canaan
- They conquered Jericho when its walls miraculously collapsed
- They progressively conquered territory throughout Canaan
- The land was divided among the twelve tribes

The conquest wasn't immediate or complete: many Canaanite peoples remained in the land. This partial conquest set the stage for ongoing conflicts described in the book of Judges.

### Settling in the Land

After Joshua's death, Israel entered the period of the Judges: a cyclical time of sin, oppression, crying out to God, and deliverance through God-appointed leaders. This period lasted several hundred years until the establishment of the monarchy under Saul, and later, David and Solomon.

The arrival in the promised land fulfilled God's covenant promise to Abraham made centuries earlier, though the complete fulfillment of all aspects of the covenant would continue to unfold throughout Israel's history.

This narrative arc, from Joseph to Moses to Joshua, shows how God preserved Abraham's descendants during their time in Egypt, formed them into a nation through the exodus and wilderness wanderings, and established them in the land He had promised to their ancestors.
