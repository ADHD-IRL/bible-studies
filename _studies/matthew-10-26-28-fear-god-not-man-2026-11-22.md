## Overview

When Jesus sent out the Twelve, He did not promise them an easy reception. He warned them they would go "as sheep in the midst of wolves" (Matthew 10:16). Then, three times in a few verses, He told them, "Do not fear." In the middle of those words He named the one fear they should keep: "fear Him who is able to destroy both soul and body in hell." This study looks at how Jesus sets the fear of God against the fear of man, and why that exchange is the root of bold witness. The fear of people keeps us quiet. The fear of God, joined to the knowledge of His care, sets us free to speak. It follows the study [Beginning of Wisdom]({{ '/studies/proverbs-9-10-beginning-of-wisdom-2026-11-21/' | relative_url }}), which showed that the fear of the Lord is where wisdom starts.

## Theological Analysis

Jesus begins with the future. "There is nothing covered that will not be revealed" (Matthew 10:26). The opposition His followers face is not the last word. A day is coming when the truth will be fully known, when the people who slandered Jesus will see who He is, and when the faithfulness of His servants will be brought into the light. Fear shrinks when we remember how the story ends.

From that promise comes a command. "Whatever I tell you in the dark, speak in the light" (10:27). Jesus taught His disciples privately, and He expected them to proclaim it publicly. The gospel was never meant to be a secret kept among friends. The fear of man pushes the message back into the dark. Jesus pushes it out onto the housetops.

Verse 28 is the center of the passage. Jesus does not deny that people can do real harm. Some of the Twelve would die for their witness. But He measures that harm against something larger. People can kill the body, and that is the limit of their power. God holds authority over both soul and body, now and forever. To fear people more than God is to get the scale of reality backwards. When the greater fear takes its proper place, the lesser fear loses its grip. Peter and the apostles lived this out when they told the council, "We ought to obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29).

Then Jesus turns from warning to tenderness. The same God who is to be feared watches over sparrows, birds so cheap that two sold for a small coin, and not one falls apart from Him. He has numbered every hair on His children's heads. The fear of God in this passage is not the fear of an enemy. It is reverent awe toward a Father who is both all-powerful and deeply caring. The God we fear is the God who keeps us, and that is why Jesus can say, "Do not fear therefore; you are of more value than many sparrows" (10:31).

The passage ends with a promise and a warning about confession. Whoever acknowledges Jesus before people, He will acknowledge before the Father (10:32), and verse 33 gives the sober opposite. The stakes of speaking or staying silent are eternal. Courage to confess Christ grows from seeing God as both the One we answer to and the One who holds us safe.

## Historical & Cultural Context

Matthew 10 records the first mission of the twelve apostles. Jesus sent them to the towns of Israel to preach that the kingdom of heaven was at hand and to heal the sick (10:5-8). His instructions look beyond that short mission to the persecution His followers would face in the years ahead: being handed over to councils, flogged in synagogues and brought before governors and kings (10:17-18). The early church read these words as a guide for their own costly witness.

Several details would have been vivid to the first hearers. Houses in Israel had flat roofs, and announcements were often made from rooftops so a whole neighborhood could hear. The "copper coin" was the assarion, a small Roman coin worth a fraction of a day's wage. Sparrows were among the cheapest food sold in the market. The word Jesus uses for "hell" is Gehenna, named after the Valley of Hinnom south of Jerusalem, a place associated in Israel's memory with idolatry and judgment, and used by Jesus for final judgment.

## Greek & Hebrew Word Study

1. **"phobeō" [Greek G5399]: "fear"**
   To fear, be afraid or revere. Jesus uses the same verb for the fear He forbids toward people and the fear He commands toward God. The difference lies in its object.

2. **"kēryssō" [Greek G2784]: "preach"**
   To proclaim publicly as a herald. A herald announced the king's message openly to all, which is the picture behind preaching "on the housetops."

3. **"psychē" [Greek G5590]: "soul"**
   The inner life of a person, the self that continues beyond physical death. Human enemies cannot reach it, but God holds it in His hand.

4. **"geenna" [Greek G1067]: "hell"**
   From the Hebrew name of the Valley of Hinnom. Jesus uses it for the place of final judgment, the reality that gives His warning its weight.

5. **"homologeō" [Greek G3670]: "confess"**
   To say the same thing, to agree openly, to acknowledge publicly. To confess Christ is to stand with Him out loud before others.

## Apologetics & Deeper Study

Matthew 10:26-28 helps explain one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the truth of the gospel: the courage of the first witnesses. Men who fled when Jesus was arrested later stood before councils and rulers and refused to stop speaking about Him, even at the cost of prison and death. Something changed them. They had seen the risen Christ, and they had learned to fear God more than men. People do not willingly suffer for what they know to be false.

The passage also answers the idea that the fear of God is only a tool for control. Jesus pairs His warning with the most tender picture of God's care in the Gospels. The God who is to be feared is the Father who notices sparrows and numbers hairs. That combination of holy authority and personal love is unique to the God of Scripture, and it produces people who are neither cowed by threats nor careless about God.

### Common Objections & Responses

1. **Objection:** "Jesus says 'Do not fear' and then says 'fear Him.' That is a contradiction."

   **Response:** The same word has two different objects. Jesus forbids the fear that lets people control us and commands the reverent fear of God that frees us. Rightly fearing God is what removes the fear of man.

2. **Objection:** "Fearing hell is a selfish or unhealthy motive."

   **Response:** Jesus Himself used the reality of judgment to steady His disciples, but He did not leave them there. He moved straight to the Father's care. Reverent fear of God's authority and trust in His love work together. Taking eternity seriously is wisdom, not fear-mongering.

3. **Objection:** "Speaking boldly about Jesus will only push people away."

   **Response:** Boldness is not harshness. In the same chapter Jesus tells His followers to be "wise as serpents and harmless as doves" (10:16). The call is to speak openly and graciously, not to stay silent. Silence offers people nothing.

### Supporting Evidence

1. **The Witness of the Apostles**
   Acts records Peter, John and the apostles continuing to preach after being threatened and beaten, saying they must obey God rather than men (Acts 4:19-20, 5:29), the very courage Jesus called for in Matthew 10.

2. **An Old Testament Pattern**
   Isaiah 51:12 and Psalm 56:11 make the same contrast between frail human threats and the greatness of God, showing that Jesus drew on a long biblical theme.

3. **Parallel Teaching in Luke**
   Luke 12:4-7 records a parallel saying, including the sparrows and the numbered hairs, which shows that this teaching was central to how the early church remembered Jesus' words on fear.
